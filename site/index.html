01Problem

Freezing starts somewhere small, at a moment nobody can predict

Fig. 2

Crystallization of a supercooled liquid begins with a rare event: a nanometer-sized crystal nucleus appears somewhere in a large volume of disordered atoms, and either dissolves or grows. Molecular dynamics resolves this atom by atom, but nobody knows in advance where or when it will happen.

So the usual workflow writes the full trajectory to disk at high frequency and searches it afterwards. At scale, I/O becomes the bottleneck instead of computation, and almost all of the stored data describes plain liquid (Fig. 2).

02Approach

Analyze the atoms while they are still in memory

Fig. 3

One MPI job, split in two. Simulation ranks step the molecular dynamics with forces on the GPU. Analysis ranks sit on CPU cores and never touch a GPU. Every 100 steps each simulation rank ships its atoms to an analysis rank without waiting, and keeps stepping.

Simulate on GPUs

A CUDA force kernel inside the course code

The SIM side is the course MD code pmd.c (spatial decomposition, atom caching from 6 neighbors, migration) with a CUDA force path. Each step a rank copies coordinates to the GPU, sorts atoms by cell, computes Lennard-Jones forces in double precision and copies them back. GPU and CPU forces agree to 8.0e-14.

Separate analysis ranks

MPI_Comm_split and a send nobody waits for

MPI_Comm_split gives SIM and ANA ranks their own communicators. Each SIM rank packs residents, halo atoms and global IDs into one message and posts MPI_Isend. It waits on that send only before the next one, 100 steps later, and that wait is timed as the stall.

The wider halo

3.0 instead of 2.5, because of neighbors of neighbors

The averaged order q̄6 of an atom averages over its neighbors within r_q = 1.5, and each of those neighbors needs its own full shell. So an ANA rank needs every atom within 2 r_q = 3.0 of its subdomain: wider than the 2.5 force cutoff the simulation already exchanges. A second halo exchange of width 3.0 runs every 100 steps.

03Experiment

Melt it, quench it, plant a seed, let go

Fig. 4
Table 1

A million atoms are melted, quenched below freezing, and held. A small FCC crystal seed is frozen at the box center through the quench, then released at t = 40 to see whether it grows (Fig. 4).

Controls at the same state point

With no seed, the liquid stayed liquid for 80k hold steps at T* = 0.70 (largest solid cluster at most 12 atoms).

Seeds of 55 and 87 atoms melted. Seeds of 249 and 369 atoms grew. The critical size lies between 87 and 249 atoms.

At T* = 0.55 the liquid froze on its own, which would have competed with the seed. So the run uses 0.70.

04Classifier

A 402-parameter network nails the bulk, and ties a threshold at the interface

Fig. 5
Table 2

Each atom gets a fingerprint of how its neighbors are arranged. Steinhardt bond-order parameters project the directions to the neighbors within r_q = 1.5 onto spherical harmonics of order 4 and 6. The result is large and characteristic in a crystal (perfect FCC: q4 = 0.191, q6 = 0.575) and small in a liquid. Averaging over the atom and its neighbors (q̄4, q̄6) separates the two much more sharply (Fig. 5).

The five features [q̄4, q̄6, q4, q6, coordination] feed an MLP 5-16-16-2 with ReLU, 402 parameters, trained in PyTorch on crystals and liquids generated by the project's own code. It is exported to a C header and run as a hand-written C forward pass that matches PyTorch to 1.8e-15. The baseline is one threshold: q̄6 > 0.308.

05Growth

The seed grew to 428,505 atoms, and nothing else froze

Fig. 6 to 9
Table 3

The analysis ran at all 974 analysis steps and tracked the largest crystalline cluster from about 600 atoms at release to 41% of the box. Hover or drag across Fig. 6 and 7 to read the values.

  • No other nucleus formed. After the release, the second-largest cluster never exceeded 34 atoms (MLP) or 43 atoms (threshold).
  • Growth like t³, not exponential. After t of about 200 the cube root of n_max grows almost linearly: the interface moves at a roughly constant speed.
  • The threshold always finds more. Its cluster is 5 to 23% larger from t = 100 on. The surplus sits on the crystal surface and at defects between lobes.
  • Cut short, not finished. The 1-hour debug queue limit stopped the run at step 97,400 of 112,000, with the crystal still growing.

06Exactness

In situ labels match post hoc labels, exactly

Fig. 10
0
mismatches in 7,104,000 atom labels

The same seeded run was analyzed twice: in situ, and post hoc from a full dump. Two rank layouts, 3,552,000 atom labels each. Both label sets, the membership of the largest cluster, and n_max agree at every one of the 111 analysis steps (Fig. 10).

It is exact because of the 3.0 halo. In a unit test with a halo of only r_q = 1.5, q6 was still exact but q̄6 was wrong by up to 1.7e-2 at the subdomain edges.

07Speed

One P100 beats a core 33 to 43x, but the whole node only 2.4 to 3.4x

Fig. 11, 12
job 14364484

The fair comparison is the whole node. With forces on the GPU, the force call takes 16 to 23 ns per atom, and the host work around it (integration, halo packing, migration, on one core per rank) is 53 to 67% of the 1-GPU step. That host part grows faster than the force part, so the GPU advantage shrinks at large N (Fig. 11).

Going from 1 to 2 P100s at 1,048,576 atoms halves the force call (16.3 to 8.7 ms) but not the host part, so strong scaling reaches 1.70x.

The kernel bug: threads in memory order got slower as the liquid diffused

Symptom
22.8→32ms

Along the production protocol the step time rose over the first ~7,000 steps and then stayed high. All of the rise was in the GPU force call: 10.3 to 19.4 ms per rank. Host work and MPI did not change.

Fix
31.8→18.7ms

Thread s handles atom vals[s] of the cell sort the kernel already does, so a warp always covers one region of space. Each atom sums the same cells in the same order: forces are bit-identical. Hold-phase step 1.7x faster, no drift.

08Cost

Analysis costs about 1 ms per step, if it has enough ranks

Fig. 13
jobs 14364486, 14362880, 14363659

Production layout, 1,048,576 atoms, analysis every 100 steps, on and off interleaved in the same job, 3 repeats each. The SIM ranks never waited for the ANA ranks, except when there was only one (Fig. 13).

RuleGive the analysis enough ranks to finish before the next batch arrives: t_analysis < K x step time.

With 4 ANA ranks the analysis takes 0.84 s against 1.96 s of MD between analysis steps, and the SIM ranks never wait. With 1 rank it takes 3.08 s against 3.13 s: each SIM rank blocks on its previous send (13.6 s in total) and the step grows 25%.

On the same node the cost comes from sharing cores, caches and memory bandwidth, not from the protocol: the SIM-side halo and packing add 0.075 ms per step, and moving the ANA ranks to another node removes the rest.

09Disk

1.85 GB on disk instead of 24.5 GB

Fig. 14

The labels cost 0.52 bytes per atom per analysis step on average (5 bytes per crystalline atom), against 24 for the dump. Both costs are linear in N, so the ratio does not depend on N. It depends on the crystalline fraction and on K.

These are bytes, not seconds. At this N a dump step is 25 MB, which a parallel file system writes in well under a second. The saving is in storage, and it grows with N and with more frequent analysis.

11Limits

What this run did not show, and what comes next

README §6, §7

Limits

  • GPU scaling stops at 2 P100s. Every GPU number comes from one debug node. Multi-node V100 and A100 jobs were still waiting in the queue when the report was written.
  • The production run used the original kernel and was cut short at step 97,400 of 112,000 by the 1-hour debug limit. The fix gives bit-identical forces, so the physics is unaffected.
  • At the interface the classifier ties the baseline (73.7% vs 75.0% within 1 σ). Both place the interface 0.5 to 1 σ into the liquid, which inflates n_max by about one surface shell.
  • The GPU path is host-bound. Only the forces run on the GPU; host work is 53 to 67% of a 1-GPU step.
  • Seeded, not homogeneous, nucleation. The run does not measure a nucleation rate.

Next steps

  • Device-resident positions with halo-only transfers, to remove the host work that now limits the step.
  • Unseeded nucleation. At T* = 0.55 the liquid nucleates on its own within 15k to 25k hold steps, which gives a rate and the location of spontaneous nuclei.
  • An interface-aware classifier, and a parallel cluster search: it runs on the ANA root today and grows with the crystal.
  • Multi-node A100 runs with the fixed kernel and ANA ranks on their own nodes.

Simulate on GPUs, understand the result in flight, store only what matters.
